These are nine part-time jobs for retirees that pay way more than you think. Most of them do not require a college degree. Many of them do not require any previous experience. And here is the part that surprises people most. These are not jobs that put up with older workers. These are jobs that actually prefer them.

Plenty of so-called senior jobs turn out to be very hard for retirees to land, because the employer quietly wants someone younger and cheaper. The jobs on this list are the opposite. What they are really paying for is the thing you spent your whole life building. Experience. Judgment. Patience. And trust. Job number one is your old job, at a new price.

Here is how it usually plays out. When you retire, the company loses the only person who knows where everything is. Six months later, something breaks. The twenty-eight-year-old they hired cannot figure out why the report is wrong. And somebody in a meeting says, can we just call him? That phone call is the job. And it can pay more than the job you left.

You come back as a contractor. That means no benefits and no pension contributions, so the standard rule is to multiply your old hourly rate by one point three to one point five, just to break even. On a one hundred thousand dollar salary, that comes out to about ninety-four dollars an hour. So the person who used to earn forty-eight dollars an hour walks back through the same door at ninety-four dollars an hour, ten hours a week.

Now, you might be thinking, Social Security is just going to take it. Under full retirement age, Social Security holds back one dollar for every two dollars you earn over twenty-four thousand, four hundred and eighty dollars. But the month you reach full retirement age, there is no limit at all. At sixty-seven, you can bill ninety-four dollars an hour all year, and Social Security does not care. The one thing to check is your pension, and that is one phone call.

That same experience has an even higher price. It is called being an expert witness. A building cracked. A surgery went wrong. The books do not add up. Both sides need somebody who did that exact job for thirty years to read the file and explain what happened.

One industry survey of more than sixteen hundred expert witnesses found the median rate to review a case is four hundred and fifty dollars an hour. A deposition is five hundred dollars an hour. And the median retainer, the check they send before you do anything, is three thousand dollars. That is the median, not the famous surgeon.

If you are worried about freezing on the stand, here is the good news. Depositions are now almost all done over Zoom, and only about five percent of cases or fewer ever go to trial. So most of the job is reading at your desk. And for nurses, there is a version called a legal nurse consultant. Law firms pay one hundred and twenty-five to one hundred and fifty dollars an hour to review medical records. The average floor nurse makes about thirty-three.

And there is one more price for that knowledge. Teaching it on camera. Scotty Kilmer has been a mechanic for fifty-seven years. He built over six million subscribers on YouTube, and according to Fox News, he has made around twenty-four million dollars just explaining what he already knew. Chef Jean-Pierre retired from restaurants and built over two million subscribers on sixty years in the kitchen.

Neither of them learned a brand new skill. They found a new price for the one they already had. And you do not need millions of subscribers for this to work. Tutoring, coaching, and consulting all pay well. The hard part is always finding clients, and putting your knowledge on camera lets the right people find you instead.

So, same job, same thirty years, four prices. Employee. Contractor. Expert. Or teacher.

This is the new economy. A college degree does not prepare anyone for everything, and new kinds of work are appearing faster than colleges can keep up, especially now with AI. Meanwhile, you already have something no classroom can teach. Decades of real knowledge. And people will pay you to share it.

Now, not everyone wants to sit at a desk. The next job is for people who like fixing things.

Job number two is the handyman.

I know. It sounds like a twenty-dollar favor for a neighbor. But Thumbtack's numbers show handyman work pays about sixty dollars an hour on average, with higher-end jobs reaching one hundred to one hundred and twenty-five dollars an hour. HomeAdvisor says the average job comes to about three hundred and ninety dollars. And most handymen charge a minimum just to show up, commonly around one hundred and thirty dollars for anything under thirty minutes.

The customers are not going anywhere either. According to Harvard research, the typical American house is now forty-four years old. Ten years ago, it was thirty-nine. And twenty-two million households live in homes built before nineteen sixty. Old houses break.

You might be thinking, don't I need a contractor's license? For small jobs, usually not. In Florida, minor work under twenty-five hundred dollars needs no state license. In Arizona, it is under one thousand dollars. Texas has no handyman license at all. Washington does make everyone register, so check your own state first.

One instructor put out a video on how to price handyman jobs, and close to three hundred thousand people have watched it. His advice is simple. Charge around sixty-five dollars an hour, set a two-hour minimum, and never drive across town for a thirty-dollar job.

And think about who is calling. A lot of the people living in those older homes are older homeowners themselves. They would much rather open the door to someone patient and trustworthy than to a stranger in a hurry. That is your edge. The catch is that this work is physical, and your first customers usually come through neighbors and word of mouth. But once a few people trust you, they tell everyone they know.

The next job sounds like it needs a law degree. It does not.

Job number three is a FINRA arbitrator. In plain English, you get paid to be the judge.

When an investor and a brokerage firm fight over money, it usually does not go to court. It goes to arbitration. Three people sit at a table, listen to both sides, and decide. And FINRA, the regulator for every brokerage in America, says previous legal or securities experience is not required.

The pay is six hundred dollars a day. Eight hundred and fifty dollars as the chair. And three hundred dollars for each pre-hearing conference, which is usually a phone call. The requirement is a college degree and five years of paid work. Not in finance. In anything. FINRA lists backgrounds like business, accounting, library science, and liberal arts. So a retired librarian could be deciding a two hundred thousand dollar dispute.

That is by design. Ordinary people with real life experience tend to be fairer than a room full of lawyers.

Now, you might be thinking, I ran a business for thirty-five years, but I never went to law school. That is exactly who they are looking for. And you would not be the oldest one there. The last time anyone counted, the average arbitrator was sixty-nine. Forty percent were over seventy, and twelve percent were over eighty. At sixty-five, you would practically be the new kid.

The honest catch? FINRA says arbitrators may receive one or two cases a year. So this is six hundred dollars a day, a few days a year, for some of the most interesting work you will ever do. And the reason they want a sixty-nine-year-old at that table comes down to one word. Trust.

The next job only runs twelve weeks a year, and the same customers come back every season.

Job number four is the seasonal tax preparer.

The average fee for a basic tax return from a preparer without a credential is about one hundred and eighty-five dollars. Add itemized deductions, and it is about two hundred and forty dollars a return. Do one hundred and fifty returns between January and April, and that is roughly thirty-six thousand dollars in twelve weeks. That is about two and a half returns a day.

Or skip your own shop and work hourly. Intuit postings have shown twenty-three dollars and fifty cents to forty-one dollars an hour. And Jackson Hewitt has openly said that no matter your background or experience level, they welcome you to apply.

You might be thinking, I don't know taxes. H and R Block will teach you. Their income tax course has no tuition. The materials cost about one hundred and forty-nine dollars, it takes around forty hours, and you only need a high school diploma. The IRS number you need to charge for returns, called a PTIN, costs about eighteen dollars and seventy-five cents.

There is no age limit, but rules differ by state, so check yours before you start.

And you would fit right in. About one in three tax preparers is over fifty-five, and about one in seven is over sixty-five. Intuit has even published a guide to the best jobs for retirees and retired accountants, pointing out that tax preparation can supplement Social Security income. In other words, they are recruiting you.

Taxes are huge online too. ClearValue Tax has built over two point eight million subscribers just explaining taxes. A tax attorney started a channel about becoming a tax preparer and reached thirty thousand subscribers in six months. People want this explained by someone who has done it. And the best part of this job is loyalty. Clients who like you come back every single year.

The next job is a heavy one. But for the right person, it may be the most meaningful work on this list.

Job number five is an end-of-life doula.

Somebody's parent is dying at home. The hospice nurse comes for an hour. For the other twenty-three hours, the family is scared, tired, and alone. The doula sits with them, explains what is happening, handles the phone calls, and stays the night if asked. And because Medicare does not cover it, the family pays the doula directly.

Rates range from twenty-five to one hundred and fifty dollars an hour, with an average around eighty-five. Packages run five hundred to five thousand dollars. A single vigil day can pay two hundred to four hundred dollars, and the family writes the check.

Who hires a stranger for this? Hospice Nurse Julie explains the dying process on YouTube to over seven hundred thousand subscribers, and one of her videos about dementia has over six million views. Six million people wanted a calm person to explain it. That is the customer. And the head of the largest doula training group in the country says most of their students are women over sixty-five.

The honest catch is that most hospice doula programs are volunteer. The paid work is private, from families who find you. Many people start by volunteering with a local hospice to gain experience, then build private clients from there. Training is affordable too. The University of Vermont certificate is eight hundred and ninety-five dollars, eight weeks, online, with no prerequisites. Over five thousand people have completed it, and the class regularly fills up. You do not need it, but it builds skill, confidence, and trust with families.

The next job is easily the strangest one on this list. And yes, it is real.

Job number six is the professional line stander.

In Washington, when Congress holds a hearing, seats are first come, first served. Lobbyists billing hundreds of dollars an hour do not want to stand in a hallway at four in the morning, so they pay someone. One company charges sixty dollars an hour, with a three-hour minimum. And it is not just Congress. In New York, one company holds places in line for Broadway shows, charging six hundred dollars for twenty-four hours.

Working for a company, standers usually earn fifteen to eighteen dollars an hour. Go direct on TaskRabbit, and the average is about twenty-seven dollars an hour. Money Magazine called it perfect for retirees, and a reporter found a sixty-nine-year-old doing it three days a week who called it a sweet gig.

And half a million people watched a two-minute video about a man who does this in New York. A man standing still, and half a million views. There is no license, no training, and nothing to buy beyond comfortable shoes. It will not make you rich. Sometimes the line starts at three in the morning, and sometimes it rains. So bring an umbrella.

The next job only exists when disaster strikes. And when it does, it can pay around a thousand dollars a day.

Job number seven is the catastrophe insurance adjuster.

When a hurricane or hailstorm hits, insurance companies suddenly need a thousand people inspecting damage that same week. They cannot hire that many employees, so they call independent adjusters.

You are paid per claim. A claim under fourteen thousand five hundred dollars pays the firm about four hundred and fifteen dollars, and the adjuster keeps fifty-five to seventy percent. That is roughly two hundred and thirty to two hundred and ninety dollars a claim. Four claims in a day is about a thousand dollars. Trainers put a realistic year at forty to one hundred thousand dollars, if you are consistently working.

And that if is the whole job. No storm, no work. In twenty twenty-five, the United States had twenty-three billion dollars in disasters, yet not one hurricane hit the mainland. A quiet year. But it is not always quiet. Unlike the tax job, which is seasonal, this one is on call. You get licensed, get on the roster, and wait for the phone to ring.

Getting licensed is cheaper than you would think. In Texas, you pass an exam, get fingerprinted, and pay fifty dollars. During a declared disaster, Texas can issue a ninety-day emergency license for twenty dollars, with no exam, as long as a licensed adjuster or carrier sponsors you. Florida lets you skip the state exam if you complete an approved forty-hour course.

Not climbing roofs at sixty-two? You don't have to. Nearly one in four people in insurance is already over fifty-five, and there are six workers over fifty-five for every one under twenty-five. The industry already looks like you. Channels like Adjuster TV show what the life really looks like, including what six figures as an adjuster actually costs. Take the desk claims and the water damage, and leave the steep roofs to the thirty-year-olds with drones.

The next job puts you in charge of someone else's affairs, and the court pays the bill.

Job number eight is the professional fiduciary, a court-appointed guardian.

Someone has dementia, with no children, or children who cannot agree. A judge appoints a stranger to pay the bills, sell the house, hire caregivers, and make decisions. That stranger is paid from the estate, by the hour.

A licensed fiduciary firm in Arizona publishes rates of one hundred and forty-five to two hundred and twenty-five dollars an hour. Even a nonprofit guardian service in Michigan charges seventy-five to one hundred and fifty. And there are not enough of them. ProPublica reported that New York alone lacks guardians for roughly thirty thousand people who need one.

You might be thinking, no judge hands a stranger's savings to someone who isn't a lawyer. They do it every day. Members of California's fiduciary association include former bankers, accountants, social workers, and health care workers. Many started after doing this for their own parent.

Requirements vary by state. California requires you to be at least twenty-one, with thirty hours of classes, an exam, fingerprints, and either a degree or five years of experience. Lawyers get no shortcut. A national certification only asks for a high school diploma or a GED, with a four hundred and sixty dollar exam. Texas asks for a high school diploma, an exam, and one hundred dollars. So always check your own state's rules. The catch here is responsibility. You are handling real money and real lives, under the eye of a court. But as the population ages, the need is only growing.

And the last job is the one where a lifetime of living in houses becomes your biggest asset.

Job number nine is the estate sale liquidator.

Someone passes away or moves into care. The kids live three states away, and a house full of forty years of belongings has to be empty in thirty days. You go through every closet and every drawer, right down to the dentures on the nightstand. You price everything, run the sale, and take a cut.

An industry survey of seven hundred and seventy-four estate sale companies found the average commission is forty percent of everything that sells. About one in five sales brings in more than twenty thousand dollars. Forty percent of twenty thousand is eight thousand dollars, from one house. And sixty-eight percent of the owners of these companies are over fifty-five.

Not carrying couches at sixty-eight? You won't. Seventy percent of workers in this business are contractors. They are the lifters. You are the pricer. After sixty years in homes, you know a twelve-dollar vase from a twelve-hundred-dollar one. And many sales now run as online auctions, where one sale can bring in seventeen thousand dollars and the organizer keeps thirty-five to fifty percent. Call it eight thousand dollars for a couple of weekends of work. And families are not looking for the cheapest company in town. They are handing over their parents' lives and memories, and they want someone patient and respectful. Someone a lot like you.
